Have you ever been criticized for being "too emotional"?

At work, your boss says: "Don't get emotional; analyze the problem rationally."
In life, your friends say: "You're too emotional; you need to learn to control your feelings."
In what you demand of yourself, you say: "Have goals, be efficient, be rational, don't let feelings sway you."

Over time, we've quietly built in a formula:
rational = good, emotional = bad;
efficient = right, sensitive = a drag.

But over the past couple of days, I read some material and books and found it isn't like that at all (and I'm proud of being a rationalist!).

One was two in-depth interviews, five years apart, with the AI guru Ilya (co-founder of OpenAI and a key force behind ChatGPT);

the other was David Brooks's book How to Know a Person (he has written several bestsellers, including The Social Animal), a book about "seeing and being seen."

One stands at the very front line of AI technology; the other specializes in people, relationships and social structures.

Yet they both say the same thing: we've been held hostage for too long by the story that "reason is supreme."

║ Ilya's realization: emotion turns out to be the brain's "operating system"

Five years ago, Ilya was a typical believer in the engineering faith: the Scaling Law held that as long as computing power, data and parameters were piled high enough, intelligence would keep growing.
But the transformation of these past few years has pushed him to a completely different conclusion:

what really determines "whether something can learn, and how fast" isn't how big the model is, but the Value Function inside it, that is, the emotional system.
He gave an easy-to-understand comparison:
a teenager can drive reasonably well after about ten hours of practice;
AI needs thousands upon thousands of training runs just to barely avoid crashing.

The difference is that humans have a built-in "something's-not-right detector." The first time you drive, if you turn the wheel too far or brake too hard, even though you didn't crash and no one scolded you, your body tells you directly: "That was dangerous just now; don't do that again."

This small unease and adjustment along the way is the emotional system at work; it can correct behavior quickly without huge amounts of data.

He also mentioned that once patients with brain damage lose the ability to process emotion, their decision-making becomes extremely poor.

We think emotion is noise in decision-making that needs to be suppressed;
but as Ilya sees it, "emotion isn't a distraction; it is itself the brain's 'value function.' Without this system, you simply can't live as a person who truly makes choices."

Ilya even said: "Whoever can crack the value function problem in deep learning, letting a model evaluate and correct itself as it thinks, will be able to build the next generation of superintelligence."

In other words: even AI needs an "emotional system" to become smart, yet humans are desperately suppressing their own emotions. Isn't that ironic?

║ Brooks: we've become very good at surviving, yet know less and less what we're living for

David Brooks received the most typical rational, elite education at the University of Chicago, all logic, evidence and efficiency. But he later realized that excessive rationality, plus a conscious determinism of "everything is decided by me," is leading us toward a world that is utilitarian, isolated and increasingly hostile.

In The Social Animal, he offers three jarring but very true contrasts:

- emotion matters more than pure reason
- social relationships are more crucial than individual choice
- character determines a person's life more than IQ

We've been taught to be rational, independent and clever,
but he reminds us: what truly keeps a person going is emotion, connection and character, not how precisely you can calculate in your head.

There's a very profound line in his book: the misery of life is doing, entirely rationally and without any love, things you don't know why you're doing.

This is the price of excessive rationality: we've become very good at optimizing life, yet are slowly losing some crucial things:
- the ability to sense the ups and downs of our own emotions
- curiosity and patience toward other people's inner worlds
- that grounded sense of "I know what I'm living for"

║ Summary
One starts from AI, the other from human nature and relationships, yet both point to the same conclusion:
"The emotional system isn't an obstacle to intelligence and life, but the core on which all of intelligence and life runs."

So what does this human emotional system actually look like?
Do we have a language that can describe it clearly?
