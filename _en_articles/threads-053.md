Human nature through Zi Wei Dou Shu: your "evil" is in hidden combination with the wounds of your family of origin
Is Baek Ah-jin (from the K-drama Dear X) a villain?
If you look only at her "actions," she really did do many hurtful things.
If you use only legal or moral standards, of course she has to be held responsible.

But the question we should really ask may not be this one.
"Did she choose from the very start to become this kind of person?"

Deep down, most people actually know the answer.
Her "evil" isn't simply being bad,
but the result of genes and upbringing intertwining,
training her step by step into someone who could only survive "in evil ways."

So let's ask a more honest question.

"If you had a background like Baek Ah-jin's,
could you really still treat others with kindness?"

The answer may be uncomfortable: most people couldn't.
Not because human nature is evil,
but because
"kindness" needs a sense of safety as its nourishment.

║ Her evil is the echo of a childhood hell
When Baek Ah-jin was five, what she saw wasn't "right and wrong,"
but the cruelest lesson of this world.
Her mother was pushed down the stairs,
her father looked on coldly,
and even joined his new partner in bullying her.
This isn't dramatic tension;
it was the everyday life of her family of origin.

Alcoholism, domestic violence and betrayal
kept her childhood steeped in fear and insecurity for years.
Psychology calls this condition
"complex post-traumatic stress disorder" (C-PTSD).
In an environment like this,
a child's brain isn't developing "character";
it's running a survival algorithm.

║ Coldness isn't inhumanity; it's a way to stay alive
The "coldness beyond her years" on young Ah-jin's face
isn't cruelty, but the mind protecting itself.
Like a turtle pulling into its shell,
when the brain's amygdala (its fear center) is overloaded for a long time,
it actively cuts off emotional connection
so the child "can't feel the pain," and can survive.

Research shows that
about 80% of victims of childhood abuse
develop mechanisms of emotional detachment or dissociation.
As adults, this often shows up as:
emotional numbness, manipulating others, difficulty trusting.

Because in her world,
one formula had long since been proven again and again:
"sincerity = vulnerability = getting hurt"

So when Baek Ah-jin puts on a sweet mask,
it isn't that she's inherently deceitful,
but the survival skill she learned in childhood:
"Only by manipulating can I avoid being manipulated."

║ Why does she choose domination rather than seeking love?
Because children who have been hurt too deeply
often no longer long for love,
but long for control.

This isn't a character flaw,
but a reversed version of "learned helplessness."
She learned one thing long ago:
the world can't be controlled,
but power can.

So what she wants isn't intimacy,
but mastery;
not being understood,
but this: she can't lose.

║ Why is that brother-sister relationship so suffocating?
Because it isn't love,
but the only safety structure that hasn't yet collapsed.

In a normal family,
a child learns "attach first, then slowly separate."

But in a traumatized family,
a child learns a different set of rules:
we only have each other, and leaving means death.

Baek Ah-jin's relationship with her stepbrother, Yoon Jun-seo, to whom she has no blood tie,
isn't romantic love,
but what psychology calls typical
"traumatic bonding"
and "enmeshed attachment."
They kept each other warm in the same family hell,
becoming each other's only safe harbor,
and at the same time, trapped together in the dark.

Jun-seo isn't a savior,
but to some degree a "bystander accomplice."
He stayed with her in the darkness,
but had no ability to lead her out,
and instead reinforced her belief that "no one can be trusted."

So when she thought she'd finally caught hold of a ray of light,
and that light was broken off by the hands of the person closest to her,
her collapse wasn't theatrical,
but the physiological response of an attachment system being completely torn apart.

║ Zi Wei Dou Shu looks not at good and evil, but at structure
Zi Wei Dou Shu is actually more scientific, and closer to reality, than most people imagine.

Zi Wei Dou Shu doesn't rush to put labels on people;
instead it faithfully shows one thing:
in the course of personality forming,
what repeatedly shaped how you see this world?

In Zi Wei Dou Shu, the twelve palaces can be roughly divided into two major systems.
Self palaces: the system that makes up "who I am":
•	Palace of Self – Palace of the Outer World (Self–Outer World axis): the core of personality and the stance with which you face the world
•	Palace of Daily Focus (Partnership–Daily Focus axis): life's focus, and how talent and everyday abilities show
•	Palace of Inner Life and Fortune – Palace of Money Management (Money–Inner Life axis): mental state and values

Other palaces: influences from the outside world, usually the network of relationships:
•	Palace of Parents and Elders (Parents–Body axis)
•	Palace of Siblings (Siblings–Friends axis)
•	Palace of Children and Creation – Palace of Home and Property (Children–Home axis)

When you also look at "hidden combinations between palaces" (an he), you'll find a cruel but clear fact:
the Self–Outer World, Partnership–Daily Focus and Money–Inner Life axes
can only form hidden combinations with the three axes that represent the family of origin.

In other words,
your father, your mother, and the whole state of interaction in your family
repeatedly take part in shaping your personality and values in the natal chart, the first ten-year cycle, and even the second ten-year cycle.

A person's thoughts and behavior are of course influenced by genes,
but just as important is
how the first society you meet after birth, your family of origin,
"re-regulates" you layer by layer.

For example:
•	If your Money–Inner Life axis is in hidden combination with the Parents–Body axis, and the Parents–Body axis holds Lian Zhen–Po Jun (the Star of Restraint and the Star of Depletion), plus Qing Yang and the Keeper of the Seal (Tian Xiang) with Hua Ji,
then how would the view of power, the patterns of conflict and the example your father showed affect your understanding of "safety," "value" and "spiritual refuge"?

•	Or, your Siblings–Friends axis holds Wu Qu–Tan Lang (the Treasurer and the Seeker), along with Wen Chang and Wen Qu, Zuo Fu and You Bi, Tian Kui and Tian Yue, plus a Tuo Luo, and it's in hidden combination with the Partnership–Daily Focus axis.
Growing up, watching every day how your mother and siblings dealt with money, desire, tangled relationships and trade-offs,
wouldn't you naturally internalize this whole pattern into your own future focus in life and way of dealing with the world?

So, she isn't born bad
With a background like Baek Ah-jin's,

her Parents–Body, Siblings–Friends and Children–Home axes
can almost certainly be said to be combinations of long-term Sha stars and Hua Ji, disorder and distorted values

It isn't that she has no kindness,
but that she was never allowed
to learn kindness in a safe environment.

Zi Wei Dou Shu and psychology are saying the same thing here:
a childhood that wasn't properly held
will, in adulthood, use control, power and extreme methods
to prop open a way to survive.

Baek Ah-jin isn't a villain by nature.
She's simply someone who was forced to grow up
in a world with no way out.

The deeper you understand Zi Wei Dou Shu, the more you'll find that
even a seemingly ancient Eastern art of fortune-telling
has long contained
a structural perspective highly compatible with modern psychology and sociology.

Perhaps this is exactly what's meant by: however things change, they never stray from their source.
