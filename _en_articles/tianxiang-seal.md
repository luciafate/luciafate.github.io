A few days ago I saw a news story: at an event in Ximending, Taipei mayoral candidate Puma Shen was asked to his face by a member of the public, "How can you guarantee you won't change once you're elected?"

I think this question is well worth discussing.

In an article analyzing the exchange, the scholar Professor Jung-Shian Li said Puma's answer worked on two levels:

the first was "you can judge me by my past";

the second was "the design of institutions always comes before the person."

Behind these are two completely different ways of thinking.

One asks, "How do we find a good person to hold power?" The other asks, "What if this good person changes one day?"

"How can I be sure you won't change?" is a question we ask all the time.

We ask our partners, our business partners, our bosses, and sometimes ourselves.

Mostly we start by asking whether the person is reliable and honest, but as Professor Li points out: people change, circumstances change, interests change, and power changes people too.

Character is the first line of defense, but it can't be the only one.

The real question is: is this promise subject to institutional oversight and rules?

When it comes to institutions, the star in Zi Wei Dou Shu that deals with exactly this kind of thing is Tian Xiang, the Keeper of the Seal.

The Complete Book of Zi Wei Dou Shu says Tian Xiang "is the star that governs ranks... its core quality is called the Seal; it is the civil star of office and emolument, in the position of assisting the emperor." Governing ranks means overseeing titles and recognized standing; the civil star of office links it to posts, documents and institutions; the position of assisting the emperor can be pictured as the chancellor who holds the seal on the emperor's behalf and makes his orders take effect.

The Shuowen Jiezi says: "A seal is the token of trust held by those who govern." In ancient times officials exercised their authority by their seals, and a document only counted once it had been stamped. Without a seal, you might be doing the actual work, yet you have no standing to decide on the organization's behalf. With a seal, it means an institution recognizes your position and hands you the power and responsibility that come with it.

Tian Xiang's Seal is a whole mechanism that makes things count. It usually includes: in what capacity you enter the matter, who has agreed that you represent them, what work you've been entrusted with, how far your decisions can go, and who bears responsibility if something goes wrong. Recognized standing, authorization, duties, scope of authority, and where responsibility lies.

This is also why the Keeper of the Seal cares at once about rules, promises, procedures, connections and image. These are not unrelated labels.

It cares about rules because authorization must have a basis;

it cares about promises because words spoken by one who holds the seal create responsibility;

it cares about connections because a seal must be recognized within a community;

it cares about image because appearance, words and conduct are what others use to quickly identify your standing and how trustworthy you are.

Many people think a "seal" exists only on official documents, legal papers, contracts and official certifications.

But if we extend the idea that "something takes effect once it is jointly recognized," then customary rules work much like a seal too.

On escalators in Taiwan's metro stations, most people habitually stand on the right and leave the left for people in a hurry. Even after campaigns began encouraging people to stand on both sides, many still automatically stand on the right. It works because everyone recognizes it.

This is the most fundamental power of the Seal: public trust. Whether it's an official stamp or a social understanding, whether something counts ultimately depends on whether it is jointly recognized.

The Keeper of the Seal belongs to Yang Water in the Five Phases, and the movement of water carries it into groups of people, into existing relationships and organizational networks. It notices how the people in a group are related, whose word carries weight, who can provide resources, who is really responsible, and whose agreement is needed for something to count.

The matchmaker is a good role for understanding the Keeper of the Seal. A good matchmaker doesn't just bring two people together; they confirm both sides' circumstances, convey each side's wishes, reconcile expectations, and finally give the relationship a recognized standing that both parties and their community can accept. This is exactly the movement of Yang Water combined with the Seal as its core quality.

That is what it means when, in the timing charts, the Keeper of the Seal meeting the Sha stars or Hua Ji brings guan fei, legal disputes (agreements being broken).

Knowing that the Keeper of the Seal in Zi Wei Dou Shu carries the qualities of institutions, public trust and recognition, let's return to that member of the public's question: do people change?

They do. No one can vouch for who they'll be ten years from now.

A good "seal" works on at least three levels.

Power needs a basis of authorization; it can't simply do whatever it likes.

What you've said needs to be on record, and breaking an agreement needs to carry a cost; only then is a promise credible.

And when something goes wrong, the person responsible must be findable, with rules to follow.

As Professor Li puts it: good institutions make corruption costly, require conflicts of interest to be disclosed, put power under oversight, and make it hard for privilege to hide.

What we really need isn't a person who will never go bad, but an institution in which, even if a person goes bad, it isn't easy to do harm.

Tian Xiang's Seal is the classical version of this logic. It doesn't assume the person holding the seal will always be upright; it places power inside an order that can be recognized and examined, limiting arbitrariness, giving promises a basis, and giving responsibility a home.

But the seal has a dark side too. It can make good rules take effect, and it can also endorse unfair arrangements. Once "it's been stamped" is taken to mean "it's right," harm becomes even harder to challenge, because it is legal and follows procedure.

The design of Zi Wei Dou Shu is interesting here: the palace opposite the Keeper of the Seal is always Po Jun, the Star of Depletion, whose core quality is depletion.

Positions change hands, relationships need renegotiating, and original promises may no longer be kept in their original form as conditions change.

The Star of Depletion keeps forcing the Keeper of the Seal to answer one question: is this seal still legitimate now?

When an existing system has long been badly unequal in "the effort given versus the return and authority received," the "contract" the Keeper of the Seal inwardly accepted is declared void. At that point the force of the Star of Depletion in the opposite palace may be stirred, turning the most loyal guardian of the existing order into the one who overturns it.

An election is itself voters regularly "re-stamping the seal."

And Puma's line, "If I stray from my principles, criticize me hard," is in fact handing the power to take back the seal to the people who stamped it.

Professor Li says the truly dangerous person isn't the one who knows they might be wrong, but the one who believes they are always right, and so thinks oversight, procedures and institutions are unnecessary.

Next time you want to ask "Will you change?", try asking instead: "Do we have a system between us that lets us negotiate or leave well, even if we both change?"

A truly reliable seal doesn't fix a relationship in place; it honestly reconfirms authority and responsibility when things change. Good seals and good institutions mean that when we face change, there are still procedures to follow, responsibility to trace, and room to choose again.
