Do you dislike Ju Men, the Star of Obscurity?
Most people's first reaction is:
gossip, disputes, a suspicious mind, overthinking.

But if you seriously go back to the Complete Book of Zi Wei Dou Shu, you'll find that Ju Men has been misunderstood for a long time.
There's an important line in the Complete Book of Zi Wei Dou Shu:
"In the heavens, Ju Men oversees the ranking of all things; in the chart, it holds judgment over right and wrong, governs what is obscure, is full of suspicion and disputes, deceives heaven and earth, and is caught between advancing and retreating."

Most people only see "right and wrong," "disputes" and "caught between advancing and retreating,"
and quickly conclude: sharp-tongued, quarrelsome, gossipy, hard to deal with.

But did you notice the words before that: holds judgment over right and wrong.
Not "creates disputes," but "holds judgment over" right and wrong.
That's a big difference!!

"Holding judgment" means presiding over judgment and carrying out analysis.
In other words,
Ju Men's essence has never been a love of arguing.
Rather, before a matter has become fully clear, it instinctively enters a system of analysis:
Is this true or false?
When this person says this, what's the motive?
What else is hidden beneath the surface?
How exactly was this conclusion reached?

Now look at the character an in "its core quality is an (obscurity)."
When many people see "an,"
they immediately associate it with darkness, negativity, gloom.

But go back to the Shuowen Jiezi,
and the original meaning of an is "the sun without light."
Only by extension does it come to mean "deep and unclear."

So this an isn't evil,
but something that can't be seen clearly, whose truth is undecided, that hasn't yet come to light.

That's also why what Ju Men truly cares about has never been mere gossip and disputes,
but that gray zone:
before things come to light,
who will tell them apart?
How do you judge?
And how do you find the truth and your position amid uncertainty?

This isn't a negative ability; it's a cognitive system.
And in today's world, this system matters more than ever.

Because what we lack most now has long since stopped being information.
What we lack most is discernment.

In an environment of information overload, rampant fake news, AI-generated content everywhere, and everyone trying to steer opinion, can you still stop and ask:
how was this conclusion reached?

This is exactly where Ju Men is strongest.
Ju Men's decision-making logic doesn't rely on surface information, but on logical reasoning and taking motives apart.
What others see is what someone said.

What Ju Men is thinking is:
Why did they say it this way?
What didn't they say?
What are they really trying to push behind it?

Here's an example.
The famous Ponzi scheme of Bernard Madoff in financial history is a classic case.
Most people believed him because his reputation, his returns and his standing in the industry all looked too real.
Even the regulators didn't act in time.

But the people who really saw the problem didn't look at the halo; they took the logic apart:
Is this investment strategy actually possible?
Do these numbers make sense?
Are there really corresponding traces of trades in the market?

That is Ju Men.

It isn't about how beautifully you say something, but about going back and asking:
how exactly does this hold up?

And Harry Markopolos, who was the first to keep digging, again and again,
was himself someone overly serious, almost obsessive.
In personal relationships, that kind of personality is of course not endearing;
but in roles such as auditing, information security, legal work and fraud investigation, it becomes an asset.
( → In the wrong place it's trouble; in the right place it's a superpower.)

So this isn't suspiciousness; it's taking motives apart.
And this ability,
in investing, workplace strategy and business negotiation,
is actually very precious.

Because truly difficult decisions are never made only once all the information is in.

They're about whether, when information is incomplete, motives are murky and the situation is unclear, you can still reason toward a direction closer to the truth.
This is what's called decision-making under information asymmetry.

In this kind of environment, judgment is often worth more than the information itself.
Of course,
if this system loses balance, it can easily go to the other extreme:
too much suspicion, too much reading into things, turning it over and over,
until in the end you're not closer to the answer,
but thinking more and more, getting more and more stuck,
unable to do anything.
That is what the Complete Book of Zi Wei Dou Shu calls being caught between advancing and retreating.

So Ju Men's real lesson
has never been "don't doubt,"
but how to train doubt into high-quality judgment.
Doubt is only the starting point; judgment is the goal.

Ju Men wasn't born to make trouble.
It's more like a judgment system specialized in handling ambiguity, risk and unclear information.
The more chaotic the world, the greater Ju Men's value.
Because not everyone can think in a fog,
and not everyone can keep a clear head
under information asymmetry.

So don't rush to dislike Ju Men.
What you think is overthinking is sometimes actually seeing the problem earlier than others.
In this age when misinformation runs faster than the truth,
Ju Men looks like trouble,
but it's actually a rare ability.

And everyone has Ju Men somewhere in their chart.
Which palace does your Ju Men fall in?
Are you truly making use of this superpower?
