According to the Biography of the Tripitaka Master of the Great Ci'en Monastery, Xuanzang once met a sick man in Sichuan, dressed in rags and covered in festering sores. Out of compassion he helped him, and in return the sick man gave him a Buddhist scripture, the Heart Sutra, and personally taught him to recite it.

When Xuanzang went west to fetch the scriptures, he was haunted by evil spirits in the desert. Following the advice of the Lotus Sutra, he first frantically chanted the name of Guanyin Bodhisattva, but the ghosts paid him no attention at all.

With his life hanging by a thread, Xuanzang had a flash of inspiration and switched to chanting the Heart Sutra, and the evil spirits instantly vanished; the crisis was over!
This miracle appears not only in the Ci'en Biography and the Tang–Sanskrit preface, but was even drawn on later in Journey to the West.
So, when you run into something supernatural, the advice from Master Xuanzang's own experiment is:
Guanyin is OK, but the Heart Sutra is stronger!

‖ The key mantra of the Heart Sutra
What Xuanzang chanted back then:
揭諦揭諦，波羅揭諦，波羅僧揭諦，菩提莎訶
Gate Gate Paragate Parasamgate Bodhi Svaha
In plain words:
•	揭諦揭諦 (Gate Gate) = go, go
•	波羅揭諦 (Paragate) = go to the other shore
•	波羅僧揭諦 (Parasamgate) = go all the way to the other shore
•	菩提莎訶 (Bodhi Svaha) = may all awakening be complete
The core meaning: go beyond this shore, reach the other shore, see the truth of everything clearly.

‖ Why do mantras "work"? An anthropological view
The power of mantras actually has nothing to do with the supernatural; it's about the relationship between language and the human brain:

1.	The rhythm effect:
In primitive times, humans were extremely sensitive to "rhythmic language." When you repeat rhythmic sounds over and over, the brain enters a semi-hypnotic state, lowering fear and anxiety
2.	The untranslatability of sound:
Much of a mantra's power comes from the "sound" itself, not its literal meaning. That's why almost all mantras in the Buddhist scriptures are transliterated rather than translated: once they're changed into another language, the sound structure is broken and the effect is greatly weakened
3.	Matching function:
In primitive societies, mantras were sorted into categories: different problems called for different mantras
illness → chant a healing mantra
haunted by ghosts → chant an exorcism mantra
There even had to be specialist mantra chanters to recite them, and they often chanted quietly, for fear of others stealing their craft
4.	Cross-cultural comparison:
In the West: mantras belong to magic, which is split in two: white magic (healing) and black magic (harmful)
In China: there are counterparts too, for example "calling back the soul" is white magic, and "sticking needles into an effigy" is black magic

So chanting a mantra is, to some degree, a three-in-one combination of psychological tranquilizer + sound therapy + cultural suggestion.
