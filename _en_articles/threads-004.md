When many people hear that their chart shows "signs of guan fei" (literally, "official trouble"), they immediately picture
being sued, crime, court, even prison.
Don't scare yourself just yet.

Guan fei covers a lot of ground; prison is only one extreme outcome.
Its core comes down to one sentence:
an existing agreement has been broken.

An "agreement" here isn't just "I promise you."
It also includes
rules, recognized standing, contracts, rights and responsibilities, qualifications, procedures, and the boundaries two parties had tacitly accepted in how they deal with each other.

When these relationships, which once held, run into conflict, break down or can no longer be fulfilled, and responsibility needs to be clarified, right and wrong need to be determined, or even a third party or an institution needs to step in, that is what we call signs of guan fei.

It may show up as
contract disputes, disputes over responsibility at work, a partnership falling apart, conflicts over dividing benefits, legal attest letters, complaints, administrative penalties or lawsuits.
As for how serious things end up being, that still depends on the overall state of the timing charts. You can't see the words "guan fei" and jump straight to crime or prison.

‖ How do you read guan fei?
Why is guan fei read from the Palace of Parents and Elders?
In ancient China, ordinary people called the local officials of their county or prefecture "parent officials." A county magistrate was the chief official governing the county, and was also responsible for keeping local order and handling the people's affairs.

This term also helps us understand that, besides your father, the Palace of Parents and Elders can extend to superiors, the government, public authority, and the norms that come from above.

However, you can't judge guan fei from the Palace of Parents and Elders alone; you need to look at the whole Parents–Body axis in the timing charts.

The Parents–Body axis is the Palace of Parents and Elders and the Palace of Body and Health in the timing charts. The two palaces are opposite each other, two sides of one whole, and must be read together.

The Palace of Parents and Elders shows your relationship with your father, superiors, the government or public authority; the Palace of Body and Health, its opposite palace, shows how that relationship is expressed outwardly.
So in judging guan fei, you don't look at a single palace on its own, but at the whole Parents–Body axis in the timing charts.

‖ The five main guan fei stars
Only when the following five stars appear on the Parents–Body axis of a timing chart and meet Sha stars or Hua Ji are there signs of guan fei.
Without Sha stars or Hua Ji, they mostly reflect the normal workings of authority and responsibility, rules, management, or the stars themselves.

① The Sun (Tai Yang) | Public standards and responsibility
The Sun's core quality is gui, esteem, and it is also the main father star.
The Complete Book of Zi Wei Dou Shu calls the Sun "the pivot of office and emolument," and notes that when ordinary people meet punishing and harmful stars, there is "the trouble of guan fei."
The Sun doesn't only look after others; it also creates a set of public standards:
Who has the right to set the direction?
Who should follow the rules?
When something goes wrong, who has to step forward and take responsibility?

When the Sun enters the Parents–Body axis of a timing chart, it tends to show a struggle between you and your father, superiors or public authority over standards, control and where responsibility lies.
When it meets Sha stars or Hua Ji, this may become
you refusing to accept a superior's demands, both sides fighting for control, someone being held to account for failing in their responsibility, or a matter that was private coming under public scrutiny.

② The Chancellor (Tian Fu) | The star of command: authority to manage and the allocation of resources
The Complete Book of Zi Wei Dou Shu says Tian Fu "governs commands" and "oversees fortune and authority," holding wealth, property and livelihood, and that it is also "the senior minister star who guards the state."

What the Chancellor cares about is:
Who has the authority to manage?
Who may use it?
Who is responsible for keeping it?
How should resources be allocated?

Many disputes deal with exactly these questions:
Whose property is it?
Who has the right to use it?
Has the person entrusted with managing it overstepped their authority?
How should an inheritance, shares, land or public resources be divided?
So when the Chancellor, the "star of command," appears on the Parents–Body axis of a timing chart and meets Sha stars, there may also be signs of guan fei.

③ The Star of Restraint (Lian Zhen) | A boundary crossed, norms that fail
Many people first come to know Lian Zhen as a "secondary peach blossom star."
Lian Zhen's core quality is qiu, literally "confinement," and it "governs ranks and governs authority and orders." Its truly important function is drawing a framework:
What is your role?
How far does your authority extend?
How far does your responsibility extend?
What behavior has already crossed the line?
Once it's violated, what consequences must be borne?

Without Sha stars or Hua Ji, Lian Zhen can mean clearly dividing authority and responsibility, following norms, and knowing what is appropriate.
When it meets Sha stars or Hua Ji, the constraints of the norms fail and someone crosses the original boundary.
In real life this may appear as
breach of contract, breaking rules, overstepping authority, disputes over authority and responsibility, clashes with moral or social custom, disciplinary action or penalties.
So Lian Zhen, which governs ranks and governs authority and orders, carries the meaning of guan fei.

④ The Star of Depletion (Po Jun) | Depleting the existing state of things
Po Jun's core quality is hao, depletion.
Its changes come with costs and a price: old structures and resources are consumed, dismantled, cancelled or ended before things can move into a new state.
In the context of guan fei, this means a contract, qualification, recognized standing, partnership or relationship that once held is broken.

For example:
terminating a contract, dissolving a partnership, revoking a qualification, removing someone from a post, breaking an existing recognized standing, or making a system that was once valid unable to continue.
The palace opposite Po Jun is always Tian Xiang.
Tian Xiang's core quality is yin, the Seal, standing for approval, certification, formal status and the force of institutions; Po Jun depletes and dismantles these relationships that were established through recognition.

⑤ The Star of Obscurity (Ju Men) | Holding judgment over right and wrong
Ju Men's core quality is an, obscurity: it "holds judgment over right and wrong, and governs what is obscure."
Obscurity doesn't mean the darkness of a prison cell. It means the information about a matter is incomplete, conditions haven't been explained, the two sides tell different stories, and it isn't clear where responsibility lies.
That is why Ju Men asks questions, raises doubts, analyzes, debates and clarifies.

In the context of guan fei, what Ju Men deals with is:
What exactly was said at the start?
Was there an agreement or not?
Who broke the agreement first?
Who concealed information?
Who caused the damage?
Whose account holds up?
Without Sha stars or Hua Ji, Ju Men can mean investigation and careful analysis.
When it meets Sha stars or Hua Ji, investigation easily becomes suspicion, and clarification easily becomes accusation; matters the two sides couldn't sort out between themselves begin to need a third party to decide.

‖ Two more stars: Tian Xiang and Wen Chang
Tian Xiang and Wen Chang don't have to fall on the Parents–Body axis of a timing chart.
As long as they meet Sha stars or Hua Ji in any palace of a timing chart, watch whether the people and matters that palace represents run into problems with agreements, qualifications, procedures or the validity of documents.

Tian Xiang's core quality is yin, the Seal.
It governs authorization, approval, qualifications, procedures and formal validity.
When it meets Sha stars or Hua Ji, there may be
flaws in an approval process, a status that isn't recognized, acting on someone's behalf without authority, a qualification being revoked, or the validity of documents and decisions being called into question.
What Tian Xiang asks is:
Has this matter actually been formally recognized?

Wen Chang relates to rational thinking, regulations, writing and documents.
It puts people's intentions into a form that can be recorded, cited and carried out, such as contracts, clauses, official letters, applications, notices and written records.

When it meets Sha stars or Hua Ji, there may be signs of guan fei involving documents, contracts or financial instruments:
disputes over how a clause is interpreted, missing documents, errors in filings, procedures not followed, or written records left behind turning into unfavorable evidence.

Guan fei sounds frightening, but what the chart is usually reminding you is this:
during this period, cracks may begin to appear in the existing rules, authority and responsibilities, or promises between you and a person, an organization or a system.

Rather than being afraid first, it's better to do what you can in advance:
write contracts clearly, make responsibilities explicit, keep important documents, confirm authority beforehand, and talk through the boundaries of relationships.
The signs in a chart are a reminder, not a declaration of the outcome.

P.S. The Parents–Body axis of the timing charts is only a simple way to judge whether "signs of guan fei" are present. To go further and judge what causes it, what kind of people or matters are involved, how it develops and how serious it is, you still need to read it together with other palaces and the overall timing cycles.
