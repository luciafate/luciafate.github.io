When you hear "Qi Sha," what picture comes to mind?
A bad temper? Impulsiveness? Or an aggressive brute?
For a long time, traditional fortune-telling has given this star too many negative labels.
But if you really understand this system, you'll find this is actually a big misunderstanding.

Today, no superstition; let's reason it through rationally.
Looking at this image, let's go back to the origin of the character and take apart what "its core quality is sha" really means in the system.

In the oracle bone script of the Shang dynasty, 殺 (sha) is a compound ideograph made up of 戈 (a dagger-axe, a weapon) and the image of "loose hair," depicting a weapon cutting down a person with loose hair;
by the bronze inscriptions of the Western Zhou, a complete human figure was added beneath the loose hair,
making the meaning of "cutting off a life" even clearer. (See image.)

The Shuowen Jiezi says it directly: "Sha takes 殳 as its component" (殳, shu, was an ancient weapon).
So the core meaning of the character sha was, from the start, only one thing:
[ending a life]

Since it is "taking away," it isn't an emotion, and it isn't intimidation.
What it points to is "a result that has been carried out and can't be undone."

"Carrying out an ending with a weapon" in itself implies two extremely harsh conditions:
first, you must have the courage to strike;
second, once you strike, you must see it through completely.
This is the most primal, and cruelest, essence of sha.

And this origin happens to explain the core essence of Qi Sha, the Resolute Star.
The Resolute Star's sha has never been an emotional outburst.
It's a mechanism of irreversible decision.

In real life, many things actually reach this moment:
should a project be stopped?
Should a partnership be cut off?
Should a system be cleared out?
The longer it drags on, the worse the situation gets.

But someone always has to step up and say:
"This ends here."
"Make the decision now."
In Zi Wei Dou Shu, that role is the Resolute Star.

The old texts have a very famous line about Qi Sha: "the senior general of the Dipper."
What is a senior general?
Not a negotiator, not a diplomat, and not someone who soothes everyone's feelings.

A senior general has only one role: the one who acts when the situation gets out of control.

That's why the old texts also call Qi Sha "the lonely star of success and failure."
This "lonely" doesn't mean withdrawn. It means that many decisions, in the end, can only be carried by one person.
While everyone else is still hesitating, someone has to forcibly turn the steering wheel.

Besides this, many people have another misunderstanding about Qi Sha:
they think Qi Sha must be hot-tempered.

In fact, the old texts describe Qi Sha with another line: "its might is the spirit of Metal."
The quality of Metal isn't fervor; it's cold.
Sharp like metal, and cold like metal.

So the Resolute Star's real sha isn't an emotional outburst,
but rather a very clear-headed decision:
cut your losses when it's time to cut your losses; sever when it's time to sever.
Just like surgery: it looks cruel, but its purpose is to preserve the whole.

When many people learn Zi Wei Dou Shu, they only remember that Qi Sha is impulsive, fierce and domineering.

But go back to the origin of the character, and you'll find the core of Qi Sha comes down to one sentence:
"cutting losses and pushing forward, with no way back."

In some situations, without Qi Sha, things only drag on; they never end.
