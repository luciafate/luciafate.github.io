At work, have you ever run into situations like these:
a colleague with only average ability keeps getting promoted faster than you?
You clearly did the right thing, yet you end up losing?
You dutifully follow the rules, but when the rules are applied to you, they somehow change?
It isn't that you're not good enough, nor that your talent goes unrecognized.

Today I want to share a sociological theory with you.
It may not change reality right away, but at least it can help you see where exactly you're losing.

People in Chinese societies often say, "Wherever there are people, there's a jianghu," a world with its own codes. In the social sciences, this is called "field theory," proposed by the French sociologist Pierre Bourdieu.

What is a field?
You can think of it as the playing field of a game, or a network of relationships made up of different "positions."

Every circle has its own way of keeping score;
every field has its own default rules, chips and referees.

Whether you get recognized often depends not only on how hard you work or how clever you are, but on
whether you fit this field's definition of "good."

What's crueler is that the right thing to do here may become a foul somewhere else.
So the next time you hit a setback, don't rush to ask yourself whether you're good enough.
What you should ask instead is: which field am I in right now? What are the rules of the game here?

To understand a field, you need to understand at least three things:
1.	Position. A field is made up of different positions. Some positions hold resources; some hold the power to interpret. Often it isn't that you as a person aren't capable, but that the position you're in simply has no standing to speak. People come and go, but positions last; people are often just temporary actors in those positions.

2.	Doxa (self-evident beliefs). This runs deeper than unwritten rules; it's like the air everyone takes for granted in this space. You can't say why, but you just know some things can't be said and some things can't be touched. That instinct of "not daring" usually means you've run into doxa that can't be challenged.

3.	Capital and symbolic capital
Anything that helps you gain an advantage in a field is called capital. For example, the capital most valued in academia is papers, citation counts, titles and recognition from peers.
But the top kind isn't money, or even just ability; it's "symbolic capital."

What's most powerful about symbolic capital is that
it can determine reputation and authority, and even directly define the legitimacy of other forms of capital.
Whoever holds it holds the ultimate narrative power in the field.
They're entitled to define what counts as good, what counts as orthodox, what counts as valuable.

So if you want to live comfortably in a field, you need to understand at least four things first:
1.	What is the power structure of this field? Who's really the boss?
2.	What is this field's doxa? What can't be openly challenged?
3.	What is the habitus of the people around you? Is your pattern of reactions compatible with this place?
4.	Which kind of capital is truly valued here?

By now, you can probably understand better that
your troubles in some places really have nothing to do with ability.
That flatterer you look down on may not be better than you,
just better than you at understanding this field's doxa.

That's fine. In Zi Wei Dou Shu, some stars are born with a particularly strong sense of the field, and you can make good use of them.

║ The Star of Restraint (Lian Zhen)
Its core quality is qiu (confinement); it's the best at sniffing out boundaries.
It doesn't just hear what you say, but thinks: in these words, this body language, this interaction, what's the surface meaning, and what's the meaning behind it?
The Star of Restraint is extremely sensitive to unwritten rules, shifts in atmosphere and relationships between positions.
If it wants to, the Star of Restraint is very good at manipulating the rules themselves.

║ The Seeker (Tan Lang)
Its core quality is peach blossom; it's the best at sensing the currency that truly circulates in a field.
Favors, the flow of resources, the direction of desires: the moment it enters, it can sniff out
what this field truly pays for.

║ The Blessing Star (Tian Tong), core quality fu (blessing)
A master of playing dumb; great wisdom that looks like foolishness.
It doesn't need to speak; it only needs to feel the temperature of the room right now to understand, and then decide whether to speak at all.
The atmosphere is its language; instantly making an interaction feel relaxed is its ability.

║ The Chancellor (Tian Fu), core quality quan (control)
Best at reading the overall situation and structure.
Entering a new field, it immediately assesses:
How is power distributed here? Who holds the resources? Who can really settle things?
Where should I position myself to carry weight here?
The Chancellor itself also adapts very quickly to all kinds of fields.

║ The Keeper of the Seal (Tian Xiang), core quality yin (the Seal)
Understands recognized standing and legitimacy best.
It knows, in a field, what the surface rules are, and what the rules that actually work are.
And it's very good at playing with doxa: using the language of "of course" to make its own position seem perfectly natural.

So who most actively pursues symbolic capital?
The Sun (Tai Yang).
What the Sun wants is to become the field itself.
Because what the Sun wants isn't only to do things well,
but to be seen, recognized and given a title.
Where it wants to get to isn't only a position of being seen,
but one with the standing to name, to define and to give orders.
Where the Sun ultimately wants to stand
is the position from which even the rules of the game can be written by it.

-----
"Those who understand the times are the outstanding ones" doesn't mean pleasing anyone; it means understanding the field first.

Some people's talent isn't going unrecognized; they're just ramming their talent head-on into doxa.

For people who can't read the field, however great their talent, it's just a consumable.
