Have you ever wondered
why, in Zi Wei Dou Shu, the palace opposite the Palace of Money Management is always the Palace of Inner Life and Fortune?
If the Palace of Money Management were only about money, why isn't the Palace of Daily Focus opposite it?
After all, the Palace of Daily Focus represents career, work and duties, which seem most related to making money.
Nor is it the Palace of Home and Property.
The Palace of Home and Property represents assets, reserves and real estate; it's where money is kept!

Yet the chart isn't designed that way.
The palace opposite the Palace of Money Management is always the Palace of Inner Life and Fortune.
Why?
Because Zi Wei Dou Shu is telling you: money has never been just money.
Money is only a medium for exchanging value,
and whether an exchange is worth it doesn't depend only on objective numbers, but on how the ruler inside you measures it.
In behavioral economics, this ruler is called the "reference point."
In Zi Wei Dou Shu, this ruler can be understood through the Palace of Inner Life and Fortune.

1. The Palace of Money Management isn't only about attitudes to money, but about "how you see exchanges of value"
When many people talk about the Palace of Money Management, they immediately think:
Can I make money?
Can I save money?
Can I invest?
Is my approach to money sound?
Am I generous with spending?
Of course you can read these.
But if you stop here, you'll read the Palace of Money Management too shallowly.
The Palace of Money Management can be extended from the surface level of "attitudes to money" to a deeper level: a person's "view of exchanging value."

In other words, what the Palace of Money Management really asks isn't "How much money do you have?"
but rather:
How do you understand value?
How do you take part in exchange?
What are you willing to give, in exchange for what?
Are you willing to trade time for a steady salary?
Risk for high returns?

Expertise for trust?
Reputation for resources?
Freedom for a sense of security?
Pressure for a sense of achievement?
What do you think is worth paying a price for?
What kind of exchange can't you accept?
Once money comes in, where do you send it?
Into savings, investments, caring for family, enjoying life, proving yourself, or buying back more freedom of choice?
That is the deeper meaning of the Palace of Money Management.

So the Palace of Money Management can be extended from the surface level of attitudes to money to a deeper level: the view of exchanging value.
It describes how a person, facing the world, converts their time, abilities, risks, resources and position into returns.
What the Palace of Money Management shows is how you take part in the flow of resources in this world.

2. But here's the question: on what basis do you judge whether an exchange is worth it?
The same salary feels stable to one person and like a trap to another.
The same bonus feels like recognition to one person and like being brushed off to another.
The same investment loss feels like a reasonable cost to one person and like their life collapsing to another.
So the question isn't only "how much money."

The real question is:
what standard inside you decides whether this exchange is a gain or a loss?
This is where you need to look at the palace opposite the Palace of Money Management: the Palace of Inner Life and Fortune.

In the natal chart, the Palace of Inner Life and Fortune represents the spirit, the soul, the subconscious, your outlook on life, blessings, ancestors, fortune and later life; it also shows how a person enjoys things, their taste, their inner state and their spiritual needs.

That sounds abstract. But put it into the Money–Inner Life axis and it becomes very clear:
the Palace of Money Management shows how you exchange value, and the Palace of Inner Life and Fortune shows the standard you use

to judge whether this exchange fits your values. That sentence is the core of the Money–Inner Life axis.
The Palace of Money Management is the act of exchange; the Palace of Inner Life and Fortune is the inner standard behind the exchange.

What counts as enough for you?
What counts as worth it?
What counts as not shortchanging yourself?
What counts as fitting the life you imagine?
What counts as not betraying your spiritual needs?
The Palace of Money Management can't answer these on its own; you have to consult the Palace of Inner Life and Fortune at the same time:
does this exchange measure up to the ruler inside me?

3. Prospect theory: people don't look at absolute values, but at reference points
Here we can borrow a well-known idea from behavioral economics: prospect theory.

In 1979, Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky jointly proposed Prospect Theory (rendered in Chinese as 前景理論 or 展望理論), which later became one of the key foundations for Kahneman's Nobel Prize in economics.

Traditional economics often assumes that people make decisions by rationally calculating benefits and pursuing maximum utility.
But prospect theory reminds us that people aren't that rational.

When people make decisions, they often look not at the final absolute number, but at the relative change.
In other words, people don't simply ask, "How much do I have now?"
They ask:
"Compared with the line I've set in my mind, am I ahead or behind?"
This line is called the reference point.
You can think of the reference point as a person's psychological zero.
If the outcome is above this line, you feel you're in the zone of gains; if it's below, you feel you've lost.

The same raise of NT$5,000 might feel significant to someone earning NT$20,000 a month,
but to someone earning NT$200,000 a month, it may be only a small change.
The same loss of NT$100,000 feels like an acceptable risk to one person,
and to another, like their life is about to collapse.
Because everyone's inner ruler is different.

Prospect theory has another very important observation: people usually feel losses more strongly than equivalent gains. That is, the pain of losing NT$100 is often greater than the pleasure of gaining NT$100; the point isn't a fixed ratio, but the "asymmetry" between gain and loss.

This is why many people take risks not to earn more, but to "break even."

When you feel "I'm already doing better than I originally expected," you become more conservative, afraid of losing what you've already gained. If you feel "I've already fallen below where I should be," you may actually be more willing to take risks, because you're not chasing more; you're rushing to get back to your psychological zero.

So what's really remarkable about prospect theory isn't only telling us that "people fear losses."
It points to something deeper:
people aren't simply animals that pursue gain.
People are animals that [guard their reference points].

4. Looking back at the Palace of Inner Life and Fortune through prospect theory
If we bring prospect theory back into Zi Wei Dou Shu, we can understand more precisely
why the palace opposite the Palace of Money Management is always the Palace of Inner Life and Fortune.
Because the Palace of Money Management handles exchange,
and the Palace of Inner Life and Fortune provides the reference point behind the exchange.

What the Palace of Money Management asks is:
How do I earn?
How do I spend?
How do I allocate?
How do I turn value into returns?

What the Palace of Inner Life and Fortune asks is:
Does this exchange fit my values?
Does earning this way go against my spiritual needs?
Does spending this way feel worth it to me?
Does this cost exceed what I can accept?
Is this way of living one I truly endorse?

So you can never read the Palace of Money Management on its own.
Because a person's behavior with money is always shaped by their inner reference point.

For example:
if someone's natal Palace of Inner Life and Fortune holds the Emperor (Zi Wei), then reasoning from the Emperor's core quality, zun, dignity, this person's inner reference point readily falls on dignity, a sense of position and being respected.
So what they care about most in the Palace of Money Management isn't just more or less money.
They will care about:
Is this pay worthy of my position?
Does this partnership respect me?
Will this money make me feel I'm being used cheaply?
So they may turn down a job others think is good.
Because to them, that isn't making money.
It's an exchange that lowers their standing.

If someone's reference point in the Palace of Inner Life and Fortune is "stability," what they care about most in the Palace of Money Management isn't maximum profit.
It's:
Can this money be kept safe?
Is this income steady?
Will this risk make life spin out of control?
So they may look rather conservative.
It isn't that they lack ambition; their inner reference point can't accept sharp swings.
To them, change itself is a kind of danger.

If someone's reference point in the Palace of Inner Life and Fortune is "freedom," they may be miserable even with a high salary.
Because if that money comes at the cost of being micromanaged, tied down and left with no choices, they'll feel it isn't worth it.
Others will say, "This job is clearly good; what more could you possibly want?"
But what they've lost isn't money.
What they've lost is a sense of freedom.
So they'd rather earn nothing than not win back control of their life.

This is the most interesting thing about the Money–Inner Life axis.
On the surface, the Palace of Money Management looks at money; in reality it looks at exchanges of value.
Besides your mental state, the Palace of Inner Life and Fortune also involves the inner baseline you use to weigh gains and losses.
So the Palace of Money Management and the Palace of Inner Life and Fortune must be read together.

Because every time you earn, spend, invest, collaborate, consume or take on risk, you are essentially answering the same question:
does this exchange measure up to the ruler inside me?

Next time you look at the Palace of Money Management, don't only ask whether this person can make money.
Also ask: which ruler are they using to measure whether this exchange is worth it?

So what the Money–Inner Life axis really looks at isn't only money,
but which standard of value you use to decide whether an exchange is worth it.
