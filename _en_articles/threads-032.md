You're sitting on the sofa scrolling on your phone, and you think you're just "relaxing"?
But in fact, you're pursuing an unconscious goal: killing time until the next responsibility arrives.

You want to quit a dead-end job but keep not making a move. It isn't because you're not brave enough, but because you're pursuing another goal: a sense of security, predictability, or not looking like a failure in others' eyes.

Dan Koe, a blogger with a million followers, points out a harsh fact:
all behavior is goal-directed.
Most of the time, your goals are unconscious.
And it's precisely these unconscious goals that determine who you become.

This is also why every January 1 we set New Year's goals full of enthusiasm (lose weight, learn English, start a side hustle), and within a few weeks these goals usually turn into "forget it, maybe later."

In his viral long-form essay on how to completely reshape your life in one day: you can't change yourself not because you don't try hard enough, but because you've never figured out what kind of person you actually want to become.

The idea of "driving change by changing your identity" isn't new (Atomic Habits, for example, explores it in depth), but this essay went viral online because he used 4 "counterintuitive" concrete practices to fill the huge gap between "knowing" and "doing":

1. Psychological insight: exposing the unconscious goal behind "fake relaxation"
He explains with great clarity why identity is so hard to change: when your old identity is threatened, the brain goes into "fight or flight" mode as if your body were under threat, and instinctively resists change. Only by first seeing through the self-deceiving unconscious behaviors (such as fake relaxation) can you truly cut the old loop.

2. Reversing the driving force: replacing "longing" with "disgust"
Ordinary goal-setting asks you to imagine a wonderful future, but that's too abstract. He proposes a novel concept called the "anti-vision," asking you first to see clearly the life you absolutely don't want. Because in human nature, disgust drives action more than longing does. Rather than daydreaming about financial freedom, picture concretely a Wednesday morning five years from now, still living the same life that leaves you deeply dissatisfied today, and use that "unease and disgust" as fuel for change.

3. Highly practical: random alarms that break "autopilot"
Many people fail to change because they think clearly in the morning, and then get swamped by daily trivia once the day begins. He designed a powerful mechanism: set random phone alarms during the day, and when one goes off, stop what you're doing and ask yourself, "Right now, am I moving toward the life I hate, or the life I want?" This action forcibly interrupts the brain's "autopilot" mode and keeps you from slipping back into old habits without noticing.

4. Putting it into a system: turning life into a game with "boundaries"
Changing identity sounds very psychological, but he breaks it down into 6 game components to build a "personal operating system." The most distinctive is "constraints": rules about what you won't do, which actually force out the creativity to find the best solution with limited resources:

•	Anti-vision (the penalty for losing): the life you absolutely don't want (the Game Over screen).
•	Vision (the win condition): the "minimum viable vision" you truly want.
•	One-year goal (the main quest): set only 1 concrete, verifiable goal that proves you've broken the old pattern.
•	One-month project (the boss fight): the specific project and skills to learn this month.
•	Daily levers (daily quests): the 2–3 high-priority actions you must do every day (for example: write 500 characters of your article).
•	Constraints (the rules of the game): the bottom lines you won't compromise on (for example: not sacrificing time with family to make money).

The ultimate action: a full one-day protocol
This method isn't something you "understand once you've read it," but something you "understand once you've done it." Find a full day off and write it down yourself with pen and paper:

•	Morning (psychological excavation): take time to write down your "anti-vision" and "minimum viable vision," and bravely face truths you haven't dared to admit.
•	Daytime (interrupting autopilot): this is the most useful design! Set random alarms, and when one goes off, ask yourself: "If someone filmed the last two hours of my life, what kind of life would they think I want?" It pulls you out of unconscious time-killing and back into a clear-headed state.
•	Evening (integrating insights): sum up the old beliefs that rule everything inside you, and set your "one-year lens," "one-month lens," and the 2–3 small "daily lens" things you should do tomorrow.

Change comes not from self-discipline, but from "disgust"
When you truly change your identity, behaviors that don't fit the new identity automatically become repellent.
When you see clearly how bad a future you're heading toward, and realize that every "comfortable" choice is pushing you toward the abyss, you no longer need self-discipline. Because carrying on living that way is itself a kind of pain.

If you keep not moving today, then a year from today you'll still be in the same place, reading a similar article, and still doing nothing.

You could try Dan's method.
Spend one day being honest with yourself and writing down your system. Let getting better become something that happens naturally.
