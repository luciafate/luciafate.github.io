What is growing up?
For me, it's realizing how powerless I am.

Lately I've been watching the Korean drama We Are All Trying Here.
(Original title, translated: Everyone Is Fighting Their Own Worthlessness.)
The writer is Park Hae-young. Her stories are never dramatic rise-and-fall crowd-pleasers: no domineering CEOs, no comebacks or revenge.
It's just that, often, a single line or a single image in the drama slips into your heart like a needle.

The male lead, Hwang Dong-man, has drifted through the film world for twenty years and still hasn't made his first film.
He isn't entirely without talent. He has his own convictions, his own creative ideas, and he isn't without self-awareness.
But twenty years have passed. One by one, the people who started alongside him have debuted, become famous and moved on, and he alone is still standing in the same place.

So, to keep his peers from seeing through his deep-seated sense of worthlessness, the defense he chose was talking nonstop.
With long-winded speeches, acid commentary, clumsy jargon and seemingly cheerful monologues, he wraps up the anxiety, inferiority and envy inside.

Because he knows no one cares about a loser.
He's afraid that once he falls silent, the world will forget him completely.

But powerlessness, inferiority and anxiety don't actually disappear just because they're nicely wrapped.
They're more like water slowly flooding a sealed room.
You try desperately to break the glass, yet still find there's nowhere to escape.
So the more he talks, the deeper the emptiness grows.

Whenever Dong-man is hit hard by reality and looked down on by the people around him, the only way he can recover a little strength is to walk alone to a mountaintop and shout his own name into the empty valley with everything he has:
"Dong-man! Dong-man!"
And then answer himself: "Yeah!"

The female lead, Eun-a, asks him what the point of doing this is.
He says quietly, "If I don't, it's as if I don't exist in this world."

Because that's actually the state many people are in:
alive, but unable to feel the weight of being alive.
Every day: work, home, scroll on the phone, sleep. Now and then you talk to someone, but you increasingly feel
no one is really listening to what you say,
no one really cares about what you do,
and you yourself seem to make no difference either way.
This is called a sense of worthlessness.

Another scene in the drama is when Eun-a critiques Dong-man's screenplay.
She says: hero films are popular not because we truly long to be heroes, but because we're so powerless in real life. We know full well we'll never be heroes in this lifetime, so we need movies to get a little false strength.

The smaller we feel, the more we long to see powerful heroes.
The more ordinary we feel, the more easily we sink into imagined grandeur.

But it isn't that Dong-man doesn't want to fight back. It's just that his fight isn't the kind that cuts through thorns and charges the world head-on. He's more like someone using all his strength just to stay where he is.

In an age that chases fame and fortune and stresses success and efficiency, holding on to your ideals for decades and walking a road you know isn't easy may itself be a quiet form of resistance.

It isn't that he doesn't feel inferior, nor that he lacks self-respect. It's just that after so many years of watching his peers move ahead one by one while the work he loves never gets recognized, it's hard not to wonder:
do I still have any worth?

What he really wants may not simply be to debut or succeed,
but for someone to answer his restless, anxious voice.

[If you've worked hard for a long time and never gotten a response, can you still feel your own worth?]

This is also what I want to remind everyone, and myself, of while watching this drama.

For people today, in an environment of high pressure, high competition and constant comparison, mental health is something that truly needs attention.

Why is it that, facing the same pressure, failure or rejection, some people can slowly digest it while others are broken by it?
Often the difference isn't only willpower.
Just as some people can stay up for several nights and still hold up, while others lose control of their emotions and bodies after one sleepless night;
some people sleep it off after being criticized, while others replay it in their heads for months.

People's mental systems simply have different levels of tolerance. It isn't that you haven't tried hard enough; differences in constitution exist to begin with.
So when life pressures, emotional helplessness, chronic insomnia, the anxiety of comparison and self-negation pile up layer upon layer, they're no longer just "being in a bad mood," but may gradually develop into mental health problems.

║ Zi Wei Dou Shu
In Zi Wei Dou Shu, the natal chart is like a structural map of your innate constitution, tendencies in ability, and tolerance.
When "something goes wrong with the body," from a scientific and clinical perspective it often isn't due to a single cause,

but is the accumulated result of innate constitution × later environment × long-term wrong ways of using the body.

Some people are born with good resilience; some are especially sensitive to stress, late nights and emotions.
These differences exist to begin with, and willpower can't smooth them away.

As time and circumstances move on, your daily routine, diet, sleep, stress levels and the harm your environment brings all build up in your body bit by bit.
And these "accumulated results" are often also reflected in changes on the Parents–Body, Self–Outer World and Siblings–Friends axes of the timing charts.

║ Psychology: self-compassion
Admitting you feel inferior isn't admitting you've failed.
Admitting you're powerless isn't giving up on life.
It's that you're finally willing to see:
I hurt.
I get tired.
I'm anxious.
I'm afraid of being forgotten.
Even after trying very hard, I still sometimes feel I'm not good enough.
But none of this means I have no worth.

The psychologist Kristin Neff proposed self-compassion,
which in Chinese can be translated as 「自我慈悲」 or 「自我關懷」.

It isn't telling you to feel good about yourself, or to pretend you're great.
It's reminding you:
can you look at yourself the way you'd look at an injured friend?

If a friend failed, you wouldn't immediately say they're useless.
If a friend couldn't hold on any longer, you wouldn't scold them for being weak.
If a friend fell ill, you wouldn't tell them to push through on willpower.

So why, when it comes to ourselves, are we always so cruel?

Self-compassion isn't indulging yourself.
It's reaching out to pull yourself back before you're swallowed by shame, self-blame and powerlessness.
Because what truly lets a person start again is often not harsher scolding,
but finally being willing to accept yourself, love yourself, and say to yourself:
"I am not a useless person."

Back to Dong-man.
He stands on the mountaintop, calls his own name, and then answers himself.

That scene looks absurd, yet it's also very tender.
Because before anyone else answered him, he at least answered himself first.
Perhaps each of us needs to learn this:
before the world sees you, don't throw yourself away.

You don't have to become a hero to deserve love.
You don't have to succeed to prove you exist.

Sometimes, being alive is itself a form of resistance.
And being willing, at your most powerless, to say to yourself,
"I'm here," "I'm still here,"
may be where starting again begins.
